01

Give each attempt a score

Extend Lesson 3 from binary increments to eleven possible scores. Here 0 is the lowest score and 10 is the highest; these are synthetic scores, not real security measurements.

A fixed discrete uniform distribution

Every integer k from 0 through 10 has probability 1/11. This law is held fixed for every system and hacker and for all sample sizes compared within a run.

μ = Σk/11 = 5
E[X²] = Σk²/11 = 35
σ² = 35 − 5² = 10
μ₄ = E[(X−5)⁴] = 178

This is discrete uniform {0,…,10}, not continuous Uniform(0,10), whose variance would be 100/12. The fourth central moment controls the sampling variability of the variance estimator.

One online estimate per hacker

For hacker a, the observations Xa,1, …, Xa,n are a sample of n scores. Each observation is processed by the three-line update from Exercise 1.

Ma,n = (1/n)ΣXa,i
sa,n² = SSa,n/(n−1)

Across m hackers, compute the two averages:

M̄ = (1/m)ΣaMa,n
V̄ = (1/m)Σasa,n²

The main variance estimate uses n−1. The descriptive SS/n version is also measured to illustrate its downward bias as an estimator of σ².

These are distinct calculations: Var(M) has squared-score units, while Var(s²) has fourth-power units. Their magnitudes are not directly comparable. Zero observed variances are tabulated but omitted from the logarithmic plot.

The first variance falls exactly as 1/n. The second also tends to zero and is asymptotically proportional to 1/n for this law. Their standard deviations therefore shrink on a 1/√n scale. The centers remain 5 and 10. A particular hacker’s estimate, or one empirical spread, need not move monotonically toward its target.

05

What do these comparisons tell us?

Population variance, sampling variability and Monte Carlo error answer different questions.

Why do the averages approach μ and σ²?

For every n≥2, E[Mn] = μ and E[sn²] = σ². They are unbiased estimators. With many independent hackers, their empirical averages tend to those expectations. Exact agreement in a finite run is not required.

E[SSn] = (n−1)σ²
E[SSn/n] = ((n−1)/n)σ²

To see the correction, write SSn = Σ(Xᵢ−μ)² − n(Mn−μ)² and take expectations: nσ² − n(σ²/n) = (n−1)σ². Division by n underestimates σ² on average.

Why useful in inferential statistics?

In a real study μ and σ² are unknown and there is usually one observed sample, rather than thousands of simulated hackers. M estimates the unknown population mean; s² estimates its variance. An estimated standard error of M is s/√n, which quantifies uncertainty in the mean and supports confidence intervals and tests under suitable assumptions.

Unbiasedness concerns expectation over repeated samples. Consistency concerns concentration as n grows. A narrow sampling distribution makes one sample’s estimates more reliable. Welford supplies these estimates in one pass without storing the full dataset.

The scores here are discrete uniform, not normally distributed. An exact normal-theory t interval or a chi-square law for the sample variance must not be assumed. Large-sample approximations need appropriate justification.

Keep n and m separate

Increasing n improves each individual hacker’s estimates. Increasing m does not change the theoretical sampling distribution for a fixed n; it makes our simulation averages more precise:

SE(M̄) = √(Var(Mn)/m) = √(10/(nm))
SE(V̄) = √(Var(sn²)/m)

These Monte Carlo standard errors differ from the standard deviations of the per-hacker estimates. The latter measure one sample’s precision; the former measure precision of our average across m samples.

Reproducibility, computation and verification

The generator reuses Lesson 3’s xoshiro128** implementation with seed XOR 0x51f15e and a separate stream per hacker. Integer rejection sampling maps uint32 outputs to 0…10 without modulo bias. This is reproducible pseudorandom simulation, not proof of independence or a cryptographic generator. For fixed seed and hacker index, increasing n or m preserves earlier score prefixes. Estimates use the same shared Welford function as Exercise 1. Only first-six paths are stored for drawing; all hackers have endpoint estimates at every checkpoint. CSV files record sample sizes, seed, estimator convention and the fixed law. A cancelled run preserves the last complete result; changed settings mark it stale.
